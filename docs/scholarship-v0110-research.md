# v0.11 scholarship: gratitude, useful absence and conditional inquiry

Research date: 2026-10-16. This is a targeted reading dossier for integration, not a systematic review or empirical validation. Seven additions are proposed. Existing SN 12.15, MN 121, Lutz (2015), and Garfield–Thakchöe (2011) remain separate sources and are not counted as additions.

The useful editorial change is to distinguish three questions: what calls for gratitude, what an absence enables, and how a teaching or representation should be used. The traditions answer these questions in different ethical and metaphysical settings. Put the Buddhist additions near the existing argument; keep the Daoist and Stoic comparisons in the supplement. No additional numerical experiment or claim of traditional anticipation of Bayesian computation follows.

## Proposed additions and insertion text

### 1. Gratitude with benefactors: `thanissaro2002an2`

[Kataññu Suttas: Gratitude](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an02/an02.031.than.html), AN 2.31–32 in this edition, PTS A i 61, II,iv,1–2. *Aṅguttara Nikāya* is the Numerical or Further-factored Discourses. Translator: Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu; translation copyright 2002; ATI BCBS revision 2013-11-30. Both short passages were read. Preserve this edition's numbering rather than silently imposing another numbering system.

The text links gratitude to integrity and depicts repayment of parental care through ethical cultivation. This supplies a limitation on extending gratitude to impersonal opportunities.

Suggested insertion in supplement `sec-social-gratitude`, after its opening paragraph:

> The Kataññu discourses connect gratitude with parental care and repayment through conviction, virtue, generosity and discernment [@thanissaro2002an2]. Our appreciation of impersonal conditions is a constructive extension, not a translation of that benefactor-centered account.

The four English qualities are this translator's wording choices; no Pali philological argument or empirical causal claim is proposed.

### 2. Appreciated usefulness without permanent attachment: `thanissaro2004mn22`

[Alagaddupama Sutta: The Water-Snake Simile](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.022.than.html), MN 22; Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu, copyright 2004, ATI revision 2013-11-30. Read the introduction and the Water-Snake and Raft sections. The preceding warning concerns misunderstanding a teaching; the traveler uses the raft before leaving it behind. The translator's introduction is commentary, not an additional utterance of the discourse.

Suggested insertion in main text after the first constructive-proposal paragraph under “Emptiness, scripture and the object of appreciation”:

> MN 22's raft simile allows appreciation of a useful support without requiring its continued possession after its purpose is fulfilled [@thanissaro2004mn22, The Raft Simile]. Our retention controls address the narrower computational question of when a working representation remains useful.

The analogy concerns appropriate reliance and relinquishment; the discourse's liberative goal is not predictive validation, and its sequence does not authorize discarding useful distinctions indiscriminately.

### 3. Specify what is absent: `thanissaro1997sn3585`

[Suñña Sutta: Empty](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn35/sn35.085.than.html), SN 35.85, PTS S iv 54; Ṭhānissaro Bhikkhu, copyright 1997, ATI revision 2013-11-30. Complete short discourse read, including its sensory and mental enumeration.

Suggested insertion in main text immediately after the existing MN 121 discussion:

> SN 35.85 makes the qualification explicit: the sensory and mental domains are empty of self or what pertains to self [@thanissaro1997sn3585]. That specified negation differs from an absent event or an unavailable record.

Use this as a close textual constraint on Concept D, not as a claim that all early Buddhist and later Madhyamaka treatments coincide. The inspected evidence is an English translation, with no original-language collation.

### 4. Functional absence: `laozilegge2008`

[Laozi, The Tao Teh King, or the Tao and its Characteristics](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/216/216-h/216-h.htm), James Legge translation; Project Gutenberg ebook 216, the inspected HTML release 2008-07-12, updated 2013-02-04. Read Part I, chapter 11 and adjacent chapters 10 and 12. *Daodejing* is commonly rendered Classic of the Way and Virtue; preserve the inspected edition's actual title in the bibliography. The year 2008 identifies this digital edition, not the ancient text or Legge's original publication.

Suggested insertion in a short supplement comparison after `sec-emptiness`:

> Chapter 11 of the Daodejing makes unfilled space useful through its relation to a wheel, vessel or room [@laozilegge2008, ch. 11]. This is a comparison for enabling conditions; it neither supplies a gratitude mechanism nor equates functional absence with Buddhist emptiness.

Legge's parentheses are interpretive additions in English. No Chinese textual collation is claimed.

### 5. Keep the Daoist comparison plural: `chan2025laozi`

[Alan Chan, Laozi, SEP Winter 2025](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2025/entries/laozi/). Read the introduction, textual-tradition discussion, and sections 6–7. The fixed edition and editor fields follow SEP's own citation page. Chan distinguishes cosmological and ontological readings of *wu* and explains *wuwei* through ethical and political interpretation.

Suggested sentence following the chapter 11 comparison:

> Chan's account of competing readings of wu and of wuwei cautions against treating either term as a universal synonym for emptiness or inactivity [@chan2025laozi, §§6–7].

This is academic interpretive guidance, not unanimity among commentators. Retain transliterated terms when translation would imply a settled equivalence. It gives no experimental support to the model.

### 6. Gratitude for cognitive capacities with a different metaphysics: `epictetuslong1887`

[Epictetus, Discourses I.6, Of Providence](https://archive.org/details/dscrsesepictetus00epiciala/page/n66), George Long translation, London: George Bell and Sons, 1887, printed pp. 19–23. Targeted reading covers the chapter's opening, vision/light comparison, and use/understanding distinction; short excerpt is bound to printed p. 20. See the edition discrepancy below.

Suggested insertion after the Daoist comparison:

> Epictetus links gratitude to the capacities for perception and understanding within an argument for divine providence [@epictetuslong1887, I.6, pp. 19–21]. The comparison makes the object of appreciation concrete, while its providential commitment distinguishes it from our proposal about contingent inquiry conditions.

Long's rendering is an English interpretive witness; no Greek collation or claim of Stoic–Buddhist equivalence follows. Appreciation of capacities does not identify the simulation's opportunity-value parameter.

### 7. Historical mediation matters: `siderits2026buddha`

[Mark Siderits, Buddha, SEP Summer 2026](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2026/entries/buddha/). Read section 1 and the opening of section 2. SEP's citation page specifies Summer 2026 after a minor correction; the article still lists 2023-03-06 as its substantive revision. These dates describe different events.

Suggested insertion in supplement S10, following its edition-specific qualifications:

> Siderits emphasizes that philosophical accounts of the Buddha rely on transmitted texts and later interpretive traditions [@siderits2026buddha, §1]. Our use of named translations therefore offers bounded textual arguments, without claiming unmediated access to a single original doctrine.

Use this for historical and interpretive scope, not to adjudicate contested non-self readings or replace the manuscript's more specific Madhyamaka source.
